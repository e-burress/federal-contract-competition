Why only one bid?

Some kinds of purchases get a single offer far more often than others. Here you can see the single-offer share for each group of awards, and what a statistical model says once many factors are weighed together. These are associations, not causes: the factors travel together and with things the data do not record.

The model: each factor with the others held equal

Each dot is the change in the chance of a single offer, in percentage points, compared with the most common level of the same factor, with agency and year held equal. The bar is the 95% interval. A bar that crosses zero means the data cannot tell the difference from none.

How much does each block of factors explain?

The model is trained on earlier years and tested on a later year it has not seen. Each bar shows how much of the model's gain over a plain average is lost when one block of factors is left out. Blocks overlap, so the bars do not add up to 100%.

After award: do single-offer awards turn out differently?

Award data have no unit prices, so a price premium cannot be measured. The closest evidence is what happens after award: extra money, scope changes, terminations, options. Each dot is the difference between single-offer and multi-offer awards made by the same buying office, in the same quarter, for the same kind of product and of similar size. Zero means no difference.

Dollar limits: do awards pile up just under them?

Below a dollar limit called the simplified acquisition threshold, agencies can buy with faster, simpler steps. If many awards land just under that limit, values may be set to fit the simpler rules. The bunching ratio compares the number of awards in the steps just under a line with the number a smooth curve, fitted to the awards on either side, predicts there. 1.0 means no pile-up. 2.0 means twice as many awards as expected sit just under the line. The ratio measures the pattern. It does not say why it happens: budgets written to fit the rules are one ordinary reason, contracts split to stay under the line are another.

Every dollar limit

The rules have many dollar limits, not just one. This scan measures the pile-up just under every value each limit has had, year by year. A filled dot means the value was the limit that year. A hollow dot means it was not. Gray dashed lines are placebo values: round numbers near the limit that were never a limit. Awards heap on round numbers everywhere, so a real limit should stand out from its placebos, and the pile-up should follow the limit when it moves.

When the limit moved, did the pile-up move with it?

Years in force compared with other years and with placebo values

The first comparison is the ratio in years the value was the limit minus the ratio for the same value in years when no limit used it. The second is the ratio in years the value was the limit minus the ratio at its placebo values in the same years. Both are averaged over the values of the limit. Above zero means the limit adds to the pile-up beyond round-number heaping.

The 2018 switch: agencies moved on different dates

In 2018 the simplified acquisition limit rose from $150K to $250K, but each agency adopted it on its own date. Here quarter 0 is the quarter each agency switched. If the rule drives the pile-up, it should move from $150K to $250K at quarter 0. The placebo panel pretends every agency switched one year earlier, so a real effect should show up there at quarter +4 instead, not at 0.

Split purchases? Clusters of awards just under a line

The rules forbid splitting one need into several purchases just to stay under a limit. This screen looks for a pattern that splitting would leave: the same office buys the same kind of product from the same company several times within 30 days, each purchase at or under the limit, but together above it. Repeat buying of monthly supplies makes the same pattern, so the screen compares the real limit with fake lines at other dollar values where no rule applies. The excess ratio is the share of awards in such clusters at the real limit divided by the share the fake lines predict there. 1.0 means no excess.

The real limit against the fake lines

The share of awards in split-like clusters at each line, from half the limit to twice the limit. The filled dot is the real limit. Hollow dots are the fake lines used for the expected value (dashed). Gray dots are fake lines too close to another real limit, so they are not used.

    Tested year by year on years it had not seen

    For each test year the model is trained only on earlier years, then scored on that year. The plain baselines show what a simple average would do. Lower is better for the Brier score and log loss. Higher is better for AUC, where 0.5 is a coin flip.

    What each block of inputs adds

    Change in log loss when the block is left out (above zero: the forecast gets worse without it) and change in AUC (below zero: worse).

    Do the forecast chances match what happened?

    Forecasts are grouped by their chance of a single offer. Each dot compares the average forecast with the share that really got one offer. Dots on the dashed diagonal mean the chances can be taken at face value. Bigger dots hold more solicitations.

    Show the numbers

    Forecast scoreboard

    Every day a batch of forecasts is sealed before the awards are known, then scored once they resolve. The Brier score measures the error: 0 is perfect, and lower is better. The baseline is a plain forecast to beat.

    Show the numbers

    Daily batches

    One row per sealed batch, from the public ledger. Each batch holds the forecasts for the new open solicitations of that morning; the forecasts themselves stay private.

    Data-quality flags

    Contracting staff type in how many offers an award got and whether it was competed. When an agency changes how it records these fields, its numbers can jump even though real competition did not change. For example, if staff start entering "2 offers" where they used to enter "1", the single-offer share falls overnight. A sudden drop in the number of awards can also mean late or missing reports. This table lists the quarters where that kind of break shows up. A flag does not say which explanation is true. It says: check before you trust that agency's numbers for that quarter.

    Market depth: registered companies per industry

    How many companies are registered in SAM.gov to sell to the government in each industry (6-digit NAICS code). A thin market may explain some single offers. A registration is not a capable bidder: many registered companies never bid, and a company can list many codes.
