Federal Contract Competition: Findings & Forecasts

An open, reproducible look at how competitive US federal contracting is: single offers, pile-ups under dollar limits, and sealed forecasts scored in public. Every number below is read from the project's result files.

Findings

1. When the limit moved, the pile-up moved with it

On October 1, 2025, the simplified-acquisition threshold (the dollar line below which agencies can use faster, simpler buying procedures) rose from $250K to $350K. Across civilian agencies, the pile-up just under $250K fell from 1.79 to 1.24, and a new one under $350K grew from 0.97 to 1.96.

The bunching ratio compares the awards just under a line with the number a smooth curve predicts; 1.0 means no pile-up. Placebo lines at $200K and $450K, where nothing changed, did not clearly move.

2. About one in four competed awards got a single offer

In five civilian departments (VA, HHS, DHS, Interior and USDA), 22.8% of 97,672 competed awards with a usable offer count got exactly one offer (FY2024 to FY2026). By department it ranged from 6.6% (USDA) to 40.3% (HHS). Across every civilian agency except GSA, it was 25.5% of 170,687 awards.

A single offer means the buyer had no competing bid for that award. Offer counts are entered by contracting staff, and GSA is set apart because its very small supply orders changed how offers are recorded.

3. Since FY2008, single-offer shares fell

Among competed awards with a usable offer count, the single-offer share fell in civilian agencies except GSA from 34.4% in FY2009 to 21.5% in FY2026 and fell at Defense from 34.5% in FY2008 to 27.1% in FY2025.

The data cannot tell a change in competition from a change in how offers were recorded. GSA's line says little about competition: its very small supply orders change in number and in how offers are recorded. Left out as not comparable (reporting change or incomplete data): FY2008 (civilian), FY2026 (Defense).

4. The pile-up follows the limit

In FY2008 to FY2026, the pile-up under a simplified-acquisition limit was larger in the years it was the limit than in the years it was not, for all 8 line-and-sector pairs. At each of the 6 changes of the limit (civilian and Defense counted separately), the pile-up under the new line grew and the one under the old line shrank.

A pile-up shows a pattern, not its cause: budgets written to fit the rules, small-business rules tied to the line and contracts split to stay under it all look the same here.

5. The 2018 change, agency by agency

Civilian agencies raised the limit from $150K to $250K on their own dates in 2018 and after. Lined up on each agency's own date, the pile-up under $250K averaged 1.10 in the 8 quarters before the switch and 1.51 in the 9 quarters from it on. With every date moved one year earlier (a placebo), the largest rise came at quarter +4, four quarters later, as a real effect predicts.

The largest one-quarter rise came at quarter 0. The 95% intervals of that quarter and the one before overlap, so the timing rests on the whole pattern, not on one quarter.

How it is made

  • Data. USAspending.gov (contract awards) and SAM.gov (solicitations): public and free, no key or account needed. Results from one data snapshot, downloaded Nov 9, 2026.
  • Reruns by itself. A scheduled job refreshes the daily parts every morning: open solicitations, this fiscal year so far, forecast tracking and the dashboard.
  • Anyone can rerun it. One command downloads the published data and rebuilds every result, chart and page: python run.py quick.
  • Sealed forecast. A forecast was sealed on Oct 24, 2026, before any outcome existed, and fingerprinted (SHA-256), so any later change would show. It puts the FY2027 Q1 pile-up under $350K at 2.03 (80% range 1.40 to 2.66), scored on Feb 28, 2027 by whether the result falls in that range. Reading so far: 1.76 (Nov 9, 2026), not the score.

Limits

  • Patterns, not proof of causes. A high single-offer rate or a pile-up under a limit is a signal worth a closer look. It is not evidence of wrongdoing, and many have ordinary explanations. These numbers describe markets, not people.
  • Data quality. Offer counts are entered by contracting staff; 760 competed awards in the five departments carry a "999" placeholder and were left out. Agencies change how they record offers (a monitor flagged 85 agency quarters with sudden changes), so a change over time can be a change in recording. FY2026 saw a government shutdown and a reporting-system move.
  • Not covered. Award data have no unit prices, so this cannot show whether single-offer awards cost more. Defense data arrive 90 days late, so Defense is only in the long history. Nothing here is below agency level.
